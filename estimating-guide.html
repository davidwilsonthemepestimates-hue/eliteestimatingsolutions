Where Estimates Actually Go Wrong (And What To Do About It)

Every contractor has a story about the bid that went sideways. This isn't a list of things to feel bad about — it's a working guide: the mistakes we see most often, why they happen, and the fix for each one.

01Case File · Of 10

Underbidding: pricing the job you can see, not the job you're actually running

Most underbid jobs aren't wrong because someone was careless. They're wrong because the estimate priced materials and labor, but never accounted for what it actually costs to run the business behind the bid: office space, vehicles, insurance, admin time. Fixed costs get "assumed" into the margin instead of calculated into it.

"We were carrying about $14,000 a month in fixed costs before a single job started. They were closer to $4,000. That's where the [price gap] went."

— A Contractor, On Why A Leaner Competitor Could Bid Lower
The Fix

Separate your estimate into two layers: direct job cost (materials, labor, equipment for this specific project), and true overhead per job (your fixed costs divided across your realistic annual job volume). If you've never calculated the second number, do it before your next bid. Most underbidding isn't a pricing mistake. It's a math problem nobody ran.

Where This Gets Fixed For Good

An outside estimator prices strictly off current job-cost data and your real overhead figure, not gut feel from the last job that happened to go well.

02Case File · Of 10

The missed line item that costs thousands (and stays with you)

One scope gap, a division nobody double-checked, an exclusion buried in fine print, can quietly cost more than the entire profit on a job. It's rarely the estimator's core numbers that are wrong. It's the thing nobody looked at closely enough to catch.

"I can't trust the numbers I've been given."

— An Estimator, On Repeated Scope Gaps Across Trade Divisions
The Fix

Build a standing checklist per trade division for the items that get excluded most often (metal flashing, engineered connections, final cleaning, parking lot signage), and check every incoming number against it before it goes into the bid, not after. Never accept a subcontractor number without knowing what it explicitly excludes.

Where This Gets Fixed For Good

A second, independent takeoff on the same scope. If both numbers agree, submit with confidence. If they don't, you just found the gap before your client did.

03Case File · Of 10

Chasing every bid instead of choosing the right ones

Not every bid is worth winning. Some jobs cost more in time, risk, and thin margin than they're worth, but when capacity feels scarce, it's tempting to submit on anything that lands in the inbox rather than weigh whether it's actually a good fit.

"Not worth winning if you just end up broke."

— A Contractor, On Skipping Certain Public Bids Entirely
The Fix

Track your bid-to-win ratio and your actual margin by job type and client profile over a few months. Use that data to decide what's worth full attention and what's worth a pass, instead of treating every incoming bid the same.

Where This Gets Fixed For Good

Outside estimating capacity means you're not forced to choose between covering more bids and only chasing the right ones. You can do both.

04Case File · Of 10

Rushed estimates: where mistakes hide

The estimate finished at 11 p.m. the night before it's due is statistically the one most likely to have an error in it. Time pressure doesn't make people careless. It just removes the second look that catches the mistake before it goes out the door.

"The real anxiety starts after you've sent out bids and won one, then starts the mad dash to figure out what you missed."

— An Estimator, On The Moment Right After Winning A Bid
The Fix

Build in a hard rule: no estimate goes out without at least one independent review, even a fast one. If your team is too stretched to guarantee that internally during peak weeks, that review needs to come from somewhere outside your own bandwidth.

Where This Gets Fixed For Good

Overflow takeoffs during your busiest weeks, so the bid with the least time behind it isn't also the one with the least scrutiny.

05Case File · Of 10

One estimator, no backup

If your estimating capacity is one person, your bid pipeline has a single point of failure. Sick days, vacations, and burnout don't just slow things down. They can stop bids from going out at all, during exactly the weeks you can least afford it.

"I'm burnt out and asked for an assistant since my last help left."

— An In-House Estimator, Carrying A Full Bid Season Alone
The Fix

Map out what actually happens to your pipeline if your estimator is unavailable for two weeks. If the honest answer is "bids stop," that's not a staffing preference issue. It's a business continuity gap.

Where This Gets Fixed For Good

An outside estimating team that only gets used when volume spikes. No hire, no training cycle, no gap when someone's out.

06Case File · Of 10

Losing bids to numbers that don't add up

Not every competitor who underbids you is better at pricing. Some are missing scope. Some are pricing below their own cost to win volume and buy it back later in change orders. Matching that price is rarely the right move. Understanding why it's lower usually is.

"These guys came in at less than my cost, no markup, just labor. If [the job goes] absolutely perfectly, they're still lower than my cost."

— A Contractor, On A Competitor's Bid That Didn't Make Sense
The Fix

Before assuming your number is wrong, get a second opinion on the scope, not just the price. If your estimate is accurate and complete, a lower bid often means missing scope, not better efficiency, and that gap tends to surface as change orders the client didn't budget for.

07Case File · Of 10

Outgrowing the spreadsheet

Excel gets most contractors further than people expect, until the bids get bigger and more complex, and the same spreadsheet that used to save time starts costing it, one broken formula at a time.

"Excel is a nightmare once the bids get bigger."

— A Contractor, After Switching Off Spreadsheet-Based Estimating
The Fix

The trigger isn't bid size, it's error rate. If you're finding formula mistakes more than once a season, or multiple people can't work the same file without breaking it, that's the signal to move on, not a fixed dollar threshold.

08Case File · Of 10

Winning the bid isn't the finish line

For a lot of estimators, the fear doesn't end when the bid goes out. It starts again the moment it's won. Handing off a number to the project team only feels safe if you're confident nothing was missed under the deadline that produced it.

"I've lived this nightmare more than I'd like."

— An Estimator, On A Familiar Pattern Around Last-Minute Submission Errors
The Fix

Treat the handoff to your PM as its own checkpoint, not an afterthought. A short structured walkthrough of assumptions, exclusions, and anything priced under time pressure. What gets caught here is cheaper than what gets caught mid-project.

09Case File · Of 10

Being right and still losing the money

Sometimes the estimate isn't the problem. Someone reviewing bids sees a lower number from another source and picks it without checking why it's lower. The correct takeoff gets overruled by the cheaper one, and the loss still lands on the estimating team's record.

"My takeoff was correct the first time." The division still lost roughly $18,000 on the numbers that were chosen over his.

— An Estimator, On His Own Independent Takeoff
The Fix

Before accepting the lowest number in any category, require a one-line explanation of why it's lower than the others. If nobody can answer that, the lowest bid isn't a deal. It's an unverified assumption.

Where This Gets Fixed For Good

An independent takeoff run in parallel with vendor and sub quotes gives you a built-in check. If your number and the "winning" number disagree sharply, that gap is worth investigating before it becomes a loss, not after.

10Case File · Of 10

Losing the job to whoever answers first, not whoever's most accurate

Speed can beat accuracy. A customer who gets a rough number in two hours often books before a competitor's more careful estimate even arrives, regardless of which one is actually right.

"The customer has already gotten quotes from other people" by the time a slower estimate lands.

— A Contractor, Tracking Quote Speed Over 90 Days
The Fix

Separate a fast rough-order-of-magnitude number (same day, directionally right) from the detailed formal estimate that can follow after. Losing the job to slowness is preventable even when full accuracy takes longer to produce.

Where This Gets Fixed For Good

An outside estimating team built around a 24 to 48 hour turnaround keeps you competitive on speed without trading away accuracy for a rushed guess.

The Throughline

The Pattern Behind All Ten

Almost none of these come down to someone not knowing how to estimate. They come down to time: not enough of it, at the wrong moment, on the wrong bid. Fixed costs undercounted because there wasn't time to calculate them properly (01). Line items missed because there wasn't time for a second look (02). Bids rushed because there wasn't time to spread the workload (04, 05). A correct, independently-verified number overruled by a cheaper one nobody questioned, because there wasn't time to ask why it was lower (09). A careful estimate that loses the job anyway to a faster, rougher one, because accuracy arrived after the client had already booked someone else (10).

That's the actual gap outside estimating fills. Not expertise you don't have. Bandwidth you don't have enough of, exactly when it matters most.
